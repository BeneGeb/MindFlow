# Chaining Habits Together

Building a new habit from scratch is hard. Building one on top of something you already do automatically is much easier. This technique — anchoring new behaviors to existing ones — is one of the most well-supported strategies in habit research.

## How It Works

Every existing habit in your life is a potential anchor point. Instead of trying to remember to do something new, you attach it to a behavior that already happens reliably.

The formula is simple:

> *"After I [existing habit], I will [new habit]."*

This is sometimes called an **implementation intention** — a specific plan that links a cue (the existing habit) to a response (the new behavior).

## Why It's Effective

Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer's research showed that people who form implementation intentions are significantly more likely to follow through on their goals than those who simply intend to act. The reason: you're not relying on remembering or deciding in the moment. The trigger does the work for you.

Your brain already runs the first behavior automatically. The second behavior gets to piggyback on that momentum.

## Examples

| Existing Habit | New Habit |
|---|---|
| Morning coffee | 5-minute journal |
| Brushing teeth | 2-minute breathing exercise |
| Lunch break | Short walk |
| Getting into bed | Phone goes off, no screens |

## Building Chains

You can extend this into a sequence — a series of habits that flow naturally from one to the next. The key is to keep the links realistic and ordered by what already feels natural in your day.

Start with one link. Once that connection feels automatic (usually after a few weeks of repetition), add the next.

## A Practical Tip

Be specific about the existing anchor. "After I make my morning coffee" is far more effective than "in the morning" — because the coffee is a concrete, reliable event, not a fuzzy time window.
